"It's unpaid, but it's great experience." Every student hears this, and the honest answer is that sometimes that is true and often it is illegal. The difference is not a matter of opinion — all three countries have tests for it, and they turn on who is really benefiting from the arrangement.

This guide explains those tests in plain terms so you can judge an offer before you accept it. It is general information rather than legal advice, and the links go to the official sources so you can check your own situation.

## The underlying principle

In all three jurisdictions the law cares about one question: **is this person a trainee receiving education, or an employee doing work?** If the arrangement looks like employment, minimum wage applies, and the label on the job advert is irrelevant. Calling someone an intern, a volunteer, or an "unpaid trainee associate" does not change their legal status. What changes it is what they actually do all day, and who gets the benefit.

## United States: the primary beneficiary test

For for-profit employers, the Fair Labor Standards Act sets the floor. Since 2018 the Department of Labor has used the **primary beneficiary test**, which asks whether the intern or the employer is the main beneficiary of the relationship. Courts weigh seven factors:

1. Whether both parties clearly understand there is no expectation of compensation
2. Whether the internship provides training similar to what would be given in an educational environment
3. Whether the internship is tied to a formal education programme through coursework or academic credit
4. Whether the internship accommodates the intern's academic calendar
5. Whether the internship's duration is limited to the period in which it provides beneficial learning
6. Whether the intern's work **complements rather than displaces** the work of paid employees, while providing significant educational benefit
7. Whether both parties understand the internship does not entitle the intern to a paid job afterwards

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">How the test is actually applied</p>
<p>No single factor decides it, and no fixed number of factors has to be satisfied. Courts look at the whole picture — this is what lawyers call a totality-of-the-circumstances analysis. In practice, factor 6 does the heavy lifting. If you are doing work that would otherwise be done by a paid employee, the rest of the factors rarely save the arrangement.</p>
</div>

Two things follow that students often get wrong.

**Academic credit does not automatically make an internship lawfully unpaid.** It is one factor among seven, not a safe harbour. Some employers present credit as though it settles the question. It does not.

**The public and non-profit sector is treated differently.** Genuine volunteering for a public agency or a non-profit, with no expectation of pay, generally sits outside the FLSA. That exception is about genuine volunteering, though — not about a non-profit running a full-time unpaid programme doing the organisation's core work.

Some states go further than federal law, and state minimum wage or wage-and-hour rules can be stricter. The DOL's [Fact Sheet #71](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships) is the primary federal source.

## United Kingdom: it comes down to worker status

The UK framing is different and, for most students, simpler. There is no "intern" category in UK employment law at all. What matters is whether you are a **worker** within the meaning of the National Minimum Wage Act 1998. If you are, you are entitled to at least the National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage for your age, and that entitlement cannot be waived — not by a contract you signed, not by an email saying you were happy to work for free.

You are likely to be a worker if you have set hours, are required to turn up, are given tasks to do, and are expected to perform them personally. That describes the large majority of advertised "unpaid internships" in the UK, which is why they are unlawful.

Genuine exceptions do exist:

- **Students on a placement of up to one year** that forms a required part of a UK further or higher education course. This is the sandwich-year and placement-year exemption, and it is why many placement years are legitimately low paid or unpaid
- **Volunteers for charities, voluntary organisations and statutory bodies**, where there is no contract and no obligation to attend
- **Work shadowing**, where you genuinely observe and do not perform work
- **School-age work experience** that is part of a compulsory education programme

Note what is not on that list: a recent graduate doing a three-month unpaid stint at a media company or a design agency. HMRC enforces the minimum wage and operates a complaints route; you can also bring a claim at an employment tribunal, and you can do so after the placement has ended. Start with [gov.uk's guidance on work experience and internships](https://www.gov.uk/employment-rights-for-interns).

## Canada: check your province first

Canada has no single national answer, because employment standards are mostly provincial. Two layers matter.

**Federally regulated industries** — banking, interprovincial transport, telecoms and broadcasting — fall under the Canada Labour Code. Since 2019 unpaid internships in these sectors are only permitted where the placement is part of a formal educational programme at a recognised institution. Other interns must be paid and receive standard labour protections.

**Everywhere else**, provincial employment standards apply and they differ:

- **Ontario** generally treats an intern as an employee entitled to minimum wage, with a narrow exemption where the placement is part of a programme approved by a college of applied arts and technology or a university
- **British Columbia** similarly treats most interns as employees under the Employment Standards Act, with limited exemptions for practicums required for post-secondary credentials
- **Quebec, Alberta and the other provinces** each have their own framing, and several have tightened rules on unpaid placements in recent years

The pattern across provinces is the same as the US and UK: a placement genuinely required by an accredited course of study can often be unpaid; a placement that is really a job cannot. Check your own province's employment standards body, and if you are a co-op student, your institution's co-op office will know the rules that apply to your specific programme.

## How to judge an offer in five minutes

Legal tests are useful but you usually have to decide quickly. These questions separate a real training placement from free labour.

### Signs of a genuine placement

- There is a named supervisor with a stated responsibility for your development
- The work is structured around learning, with a defined project and an end date
- It is tied to your course, or the hours genuinely flex around your academic calendar
- You are shadowing, observing and being taught, not carrying a queue of production work
- Someone can tell you what the last person in the role went on to do

### Signs you are being used as unpaid staff

- You would be covering work a departing paid employee used to do
- The hours are full time, the duration is open-ended, or it keeps getting extended
- There is no training plan and no supervisor who owns your development
- You are told the role "could become paid" based on performance, with nothing in writing
- You are asked to invoice as a self-employed contractor at zero or near-zero rates, which is a common way of dressing up unlawful unpaid work

<div class="callout callout--warning">
<p class="callout-title">The expenses-only trap</p>
<p>"Expenses paid" is not pay. Reimbursing your travel and lunch does not discharge a minimum wage obligation in the UK, and it does not make you a volunteer rather than a worker. If anything, a fixed weekly "expenses" payment that does not track actual receipts looks like disguised wages, and in the UK it is treated as such.</p>
</div>

## The practical argument against taking one anyway

Set the law aside for a moment. Unpaid internships are also a weak career bet, for three reasons worth knowing before you accept one out of fear of missing out.

They select for wealth rather than ability, which is why so many employers have moved away from them — meaning the organisations still offering unpaid work are increasingly the ones with the least structured programmes. They tend to be poorly supervised, because an organisation that will not fund a salary rarely funds proper mentoring either. And they are weaker on a resume than people assume: what strengthens an application is a specific, measurable contribution, which an unstructured placement is the least likely to generate.

A paid eight-week internship where you shipped one defined project will do more for your next application than six unpaid months of unspecified assistance.

## If you are already in one

If you believe you have been working unlawfully without pay, you generally have a route to recover it, and you do not have to wait until the placement ends.

- **US**: file a complaint with the DOL Wage and Hour Division, or consult your state labour agency, which may offer stronger protections
- **UK**: complain to HMRC, which can investigate and recover arrears, or bring an employment tribunal claim for unlawful deduction from wages. Time limits apply — generally three months less one day from the deduction — so act promptly
- **Canada**: contact your provincial employment standards branch, or the federal Labour Program for federally regulated sectors

Keep records: the hours you worked, the tasks you were given, who supervised you, and any written communication about pay. Contemporaneous evidence is what these claims turn on.

One last point. Nothing here is a substitute for advice on your own circumstances, and the rules — particularly in Canada and on the UK Graduate route — are revised more often than students expect. Check the official source before you act, and if real money is at stake, get advice from a qualified employment adviser or your students' union, which will usually provide it free.
