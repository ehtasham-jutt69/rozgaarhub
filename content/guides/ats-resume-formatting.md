There is a whole industry built on scaring applicants about applicant tracking systems. Pay for a scan, the pitch goes, or the robot will reject you before a human ever sees your name. Most of it is wrong, and some of the advice it produces actively damages resumes.

What is true is narrower and more useful: ATS software is mainly a **database with a workflow attached**, and its one genuine weakness is parsing — turning your document into structured fields. Formatting choices that break parsing really do cost you. Everything else is largely myth.

## What an ATS is for

An applicant tracking system exists to let a recruiting team receive hundreds of applications, store them, search them, move candidates through stages, and keep an audit trail for compliance. Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, SmartRecruiters, Taleo and Ashby are all variations on that.

When you submit a resume, roughly this happens:

1. The file is stored
2. A parser reads it and tries to populate structured fields — name, contact details, employers, job titles, dates, education, skills
3. Your application joins a queue, usually sorted by date applied
4. A recruiter opens the queue, searches or filters it, and reads candidates

Step 2 is where formatting matters. Steps 3 and 4 are where the myths come from.

## The myths worth dropping

### Myth: the ATS scores you and auto-rejects below a threshold

Almost no employer runs automatic rejection on a resume-match score, and in most of these systems it is not even a default feature. Knockout questions do cause automatic rejection — "Are you legally authorised to work in the United States?", "Do you have a valid driving licence?" — but those are your answers to explicit application questions, not an opinion the software formed about your resume. Answer those questions carefully; they are the real automated filter.

What recruiters actually do is search. They type `SQL` or `CPA` or `Python` into the candidate search, filter by location and date, and read what comes back. That is a meaningful distinction, because it changes what you optimise for: you want your real skills to be **findable in context**, not a keyword score maximised.

### Myth: third-party "ATS scan" scores reflect what employers see

The score a resume-scanning website gives you is generated by that website. Employers do not see it. It is not a number that exists inside Workday or Greenhouse. These tools can be mildly useful as a parsing check — upload your resume and see whether your job titles and dates came out in the right fields — but the grade itself is marketing.

### Myth: PDFs do not work

Modern parsers handle PDFs fine, provided the PDF contains a real text layer. The actual failure case is a PDF that is a scanned or exported *image* of a document, which contains no extractable text at all. If you can select and copy the text in your PDF, a parser can read it. If you cannot, nothing can.

If an application form explicitly asks for .doc or .docx, follow the instruction — some older configurations do prefer it. Otherwise a text-based PDF is the safer choice because it preserves your layout.

### Myth: you should stuff keywords, including invisible ones

White text on a white background, or a block of keywords in 1pt font, is a trick from 2010 that now mostly backfires. Parsers extract the text regardless of its colour, so the recruiter sees a nonsense block in the parsed profile. Several large employers treat it as deliberate misrepresentation and reject on that basis. Do not do it.

## What genuinely breaks parsing

This is the short list that actually matters.

### Multiple columns

A two-column layout is the single most common cause of mangled parsing. The parser reads in a linear order and can interleave your sidebar with your main content, producing a profile where your skills list is spliced through your employment history. Elegant two-column templates from design marketplaces are the worst offenders.

Use a **single-column layout**. You can still have a clean, designed resume — just stack it.

### Critical information in headers and footers

Some parsers skip document headers and footers entirely. If your name, email and phone number live only in the header, they may not populate the contact fields, and a recruiter looking at a parsed profile with no email is not going to hunt for the original file.

Put your contact details in the **body** of the document, at the top.

### Tables and text boxes

Table cells and text boxes parse unpredictably — sometimes cleanly, sometimes as a single run-on string, sometimes not at all. Do not place employment history, dates or contact details inside them. Simple tables for things like a skills grid are lower risk, but if a detail is important, keep it in ordinary paragraph or bullet text.

A caveat worth noting: tables inside a resume are a parsing risk, but a table in an article like this one is just a table. The constraint applies to the document you upload.

### Images, icons and graphics carrying meaning

Skill rating bars, icon-only contact details and logo graphics contain no text. A phone icon next to your number is harmless. A graphic that is the only indication you speak French is invisible to the system — and to a recruiter skimming a parsed profile.

### Non-standard section headings

Parsers look for conventional headings. "Work Experience", "Education", "Skills" and "Projects" are recognised. "My Journey", "Where I've Made an Impact" and "Toolbox" are not reliably mapped to anything. Keep the headings boring; spend your originality on the bullets underneath.

### Unusual date formats

Write `Jun 2025 – Aug 2025` or `06/2025 – 08/2025`. Avoid `Summer '25`, seasons alone, or date ranges written only in prose. Parsers use dates to compute experience duration, and unparseable dates sometimes produce a profile that looks like you have no history at all.

### Fancy fonts and glyph bullets

Stick to widely available fonts — Calibri, Arial, Helvetica, Georgia, Garamond, Lato, Source Sans. For bullets use a standard bullet character; decorative glyphs occasionally extract as question marks or boxes, which looks careless in the parsed text.

## How to use keywords honestly

Keywords do matter, because recruiters search. The right approach is mirroring, not stuffing.

**Read the job advert and use its vocabulary where it is genuinely yours.** If the advert says "financial modelling" and your resume says "built valuation models", add the phrase the employer used. If the advert asks for "Power BI" and you have only used Tableau, do not write Power BI. You will be found for a search you cannot survive.

**Spell out acronyms once, then use the short form.** Different recruiters search different ways. `Certified Public Accountant (CPA)` is findable by both. The same applies to `Search Engine Optimisation (SEO)` and `Applicant Tracking System (ATS)`.

**Put skills in context as well as in a list.** A standalone Skills section helps search. A bullet that says "Rebuilt the weekly channel report in SQL, cutting preparation from four hours to twenty minutes" proves the skill. Do both: the list makes you findable, the bullet makes you credible.

**Watch regional spelling.** A UK employer may search "organisation" and a US employer "organization". Match the market you are applying into.

## A format that parses cleanly and still reads well

Nothing here requires an ugly resume. This set of constraints is compatible with a genuinely well-designed document:

- Single column, one page for a first job
- Name as the largest text, in the body of the document
- Contact line directly beneath: city, email, phone, portfolio link
- Standard headings: Education, Projects or Relevant Experience, Employment, Skills
- Consistent date format on the right, job titles and employers in ordinary text
- 10–11pt body text, margins of at least 1.5cm, generous spacing between sections
- Saved as a text-based PDF named `Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf`

Typography, hierarchy and whitespace are where your design effort should go. They cost nothing in parse reliability and they are what makes a human want to keep reading.

## Testing it yourself in two minutes

You do not need to pay for a scan. Two checks catch nearly everything:

1. **The copy-paste test.** Open your PDF, select all, copy, and paste into a plain text editor. Read what comes out. If sections are interleaved, your columns are a problem. If text is missing, it is in an image, a text box or a header. If the order is wrong, so is your layout. What you see is approximately what the parser sees.

2. **The six-second test.** Hand the printed page to someone for six seconds, take it back, and ask what role you are applying for and what you have done. If they cannot answer, the problem is not the ATS — it is the top third of your resume.

The second test is the one that decides outcomes. Parsing is a floor you need to clear, not a strategy.
