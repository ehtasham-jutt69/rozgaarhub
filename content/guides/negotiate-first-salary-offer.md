The fear is always the same: ask for more money and the offer gets withdrawn. In practice that almost never happens. What does happen is that people accept the first number out of relief, and then spend several years compounding from a lower base — because most raises are percentages of what you are already on.

The honest version of the advice, though, has conditions attached. There are situations where negotiating a graduate offer is genuinely pointless, and knowing which situation you are in is most of the skill.

## First, work out whether there is room

Not every entry-level offer is negotiable, and asking where there is visibly no flexibility wastes a little goodwill for nothing.

### Usually fixed

- **Structured graduate schemes at large employers.** If a bank, Big Four firm or major engineering consultancy is hiring 200 graduates onto an advertised band, your salary is a published number and the recruiter has no authority to vary it. Negotiating here is close to futile
- **Public sector and unionised roles.** Pay scales are set by grade and step, often published, and the hiring manager cannot move you off the scale
- **Formal internship programmes** with a cohort rate

### Usually negotiable

- **Individual hires at small and mid-size companies.** If you are the only person being hired into this role, somebody has discretion
- **Any role where the advert gave a range.** A range exists to be positioned within. If the range is £32,000–38,000 and they offered £33,000, there is £5,000 of declared room
- **Roles where you hold a specific, scarce skill** the job description emphasised
- **Any offer where you have a competing offer.** This is the strongest position you will ever be in, and it is the one time the balance genuinely shifts

## Research a number you can actually defend

"I'd like more" is not a case. A defensible number comes from evidence, and you need it before the conversation, not during.

**Use pay transparency where it exists.** A growing number of US jurisdictions require employers to publish salary ranges in job postings — Colorado, California, New York State and City, Washington, Illinois and others, with the list expanding. That means for a role in those markets you can often find comparable ranges simply by reading competitors' live adverts. The UK has no equivalent requirement, which makes advertised ranges on job boards and sector salary surveys more important there.

**Look at three sources, not one.** Live job adverts for the same title in the same city; published graduate salary surveys from your national body or university careers service; and your university's own destination data, which is specific to your course and often more accurate than aggregate sites for entry level.

**Normalise for location and sector.** A software role in San Francisco, Austin and Manchester are three different numbers for the same work. A data analyst in investment banking and in local government are three different numbers again. Compare like with like, or your figure will be easy to dismiss.

**Be careful with crowd-sourced sites.** Self-reported aggregators skew towards people pleased enough with their pay to report it, and often conflate total compensation with base. Use them for shape, not precision.

What you want at the end of this is a single sentence: *roles of this title, at this level, in this city, in this sector, are advertising between X and Y.* That sentence is your case.

## Decide three numbers before you speak

Write them down. Having them pre-decided is what stops you improvising badly under mild social pressure.

- **Your target** — the top of what the evidence supports, which is what you will ask for
- **Your realistic landing point** — where you expect to end up
- **Your walk-away** — the figure below which you would decline, taking cost of living, commuting and your other options into account

If a company's best offer sits below your walk-away, you have learned something useful rather than failed at negotiating.

## Do not name a number first if you can avoid it

Early in a process you will often be asked your expectations. Deflect once, politely, then answer with a researched range if pressed — and make sure the bottom of your range is a number you would be content with, because that is where you will land.

> "I'd rather understand the scope a bit better first. Could you tell me the band you've set for the role?"

If they insist:

> "Based on what similar analyst roles in Manchester are advertising, I've been working to a range of £34,000 to £40,000. I'm flexible depending on the rest of the package."

Two notes. In several US jurisdictions it is now unlawful for an employer to ask about your **salary history** — your current or previous pay — although they may still ask about expectations. You never have to disclose your history, and declining is unremarkable. And never inflate a competing offer: it is checkable, it occasionally gets checked, and discovery costs you the offer outright.

## The actual conversation

Negotiate on the phone or on a call if you can, in writing if that is how the process is running. Be warm, be brief, make one clear ask, and do not apologise.

A structure that works:

1. **Accept the role enthusiastically.** Make clear you want the job. This is not a bluff — it is the context that makes the ask collaborative rather than adversarial
2. **State the ask, with the evidence, in one breath**
3. **Stop talking**

> "Thank you — I'm genuinely pleased, and this is the role I want. There's one thing I'd like to discuss before I accept. Looking at what comparable analyst roles in the city are advertising, the range I'm seeing is £34,000 to £40,000, and with the SQL and dashboard work I did on placement I'd hoped to be nearer £38,000. Is there room to move on the base?"

That is the whole script. The silence after the question is the important part; let them fill it.

### What not to say

- **Anything about your personal costs.** Rent, loans and travel are real, but they are not reasons the business will pay more, and they shift the conversation from your value to your circumstances
- **"I deserve"** — replace with what the market pays and what you bring
- **An ultimatum you will not honour.** Threatening to walk when you intend to accept is the one move that genuinely does damage, because recruiters remember it
- **A long justification.** Over-explaining signals you expect a no. One sentence of evidence is stronger than four

## When the salary truly cannot move

Often the base is fixed. The package rarely is, and these are the things graduates routinely forget to ask about — several are worth more in cash terms than the £1,500 you were arguing over.

- **Signing or relocation bonus** — frequently comes from a different budget than salary, which is exactly why it is easier to approve
- **Start date** — a later start to travel, or an earlier one to start earning, is usually free for them to grant
- **An early salary review** — "would you be open to a review at six months rather than twelve?" is a strong fallback, and often agreed
- **Professional qualification funding** — ACCA, CIMA, CFA, CPA, actuarial exams, or chartered engineering registration, including the exam fees, materials and **study leave**. For a finance or engineering graduate this can be worth several thousand a year
- **Annual learning or conference budget**
- **Holiday allowance**, where the employer offers purchased-leave schemes
- **Hybrid or remote days**, which translate directly into saved commuting cost
- **Pension contribution**, which is real compensation people reliably undervalue at 22
- **Job title**, which costs the employer nothing now and affects how your next move is read

Ask for the agreed outcome **in writing** before you accept, whatever it is. A verbal promise of a six-month review from a manager who has left by month five is worth nothing.

<p>Negotiate once, covering everything you care about in a single conversation. Coming back three times with new requests after each concession is the behaviour that genuinely sours an offer — it reads as bad faith and it exhausts the goodwill of the person advocating for you internally.</p>

## Getting a no

Most first-job negotiations end with either a small movement or a clear no. A no is not a problem, and it is not a reason to feel foolish for asking.

> "That's completely understood, thank you for checking. I'm happy to accept at £33,000. Could we note a review at six months?"

You have lost nothing. Recruiters negotiate constantly; a single well-evidenced, politely made ask does not register as presumptuous. The scenario people fear — offer rescinded for asking once, reasonably — is rare enough that it should not drive your decision. What is common is accepting immediately, discovering six months later that a colleague hired the same month is on more, and carrying that gap through every subsequent percentage raise.

## Afterwards

Once it is agreed, get the full written offer, read the whole thing including notice period, probation terms, any clawback on a signing bonus, and anything about intellectual property if you have side projects. Then accept properly, in writing, and withdraw from your other processes politely — those recruiters will be in the market again in two years, and so will you.

And note the number down somewhere with the date. In three years, when you are negotiating your second job, your own history is the single most useful data point you will have.
