The hardest part of job hunting as an international student is not the applications. It is working out which roles you are actually allowed to take, and by when you need permission. Get the sequence wrong and you can lose an offer you had already won — or, worse, fall out of status.

This guide maps the main routes in the three countries we cover. Treat it as orientation, not advice: these rules are detailed, they are revised regularly, and the version that matters is the one on the official website on the day you apply. Your institution's international student office is the right place to confirm your own position, and their advice is free.

## United States: F-1 students

Most international students in the US hold an F-1 visa. Working on F-1 is permitted, but only through specific authorisations, and the distinction between them matters.

### On-campus employment

Generally permitted up to 20 hours a week while school is in session, and full time during official breaks, without separate authorisation beyond your status. Check with your international office, since conditions vary by institution.

### CPT — Curricular Practical Training

CPT is for work that is **an integral part of your curriculum** — a required placement, a credit-bearing internship, or a co-op term that your programme recognises.

- Authorised by your **Designated School Official (DSO)**, who records it on your I-20. There is no application to USCIS and no separate work permit card
- Must be directly related to your major and tied to your programme
- Available **before** you complete your degree
- Generally requires you to have been enrolled full time for one academic year, with narrow exceptions for graduate programmes where the placement is required from the start
- Can be part time (20 hours or fewer a week) or full time

The trap: **12 months or more of full-time CPT eliminates your eligibility for OPT.** Part-time CPT does not have this effect, and neither does full-time CPT totalling under twelve months. If you are considering multiple co-op terms, track the cumulative total carefully before you commit.

Because CPT is authorised in-house, it is often much faster than OPT — but it must be authorised *before* you start work. Not on your first day. Before.

### OPT — Optional Practical Training

OPT gives **12 months** of work authorisation in your field of study, available either before completing your degree (pre-completion) or after (post-completion). Most people use post-completion OPT.

- Requires filing **Form I-765** with USCIS and receiving an **Employment Authorization Document (EAD)** before you can start work
- Your DSO must first recommend it and update your SEVIS record
- Processing takes time, and it has been volatile — plan on months, not weeks, and file as early as your window allows
- Post-completion OPT applications have a filing window around your programme end date; missing it is not usually recoverable
- There are limits on how long you may be unemployed during OPT. Exceeding them puts your status at risk, so report employment to your DSO promptly

### STEM OPT extension

If your degree is on the DHS STEM Designated Degree Program List, you may apply for a **24-month extension**, giving up to 36 months in total. The employer must be enrolled in E-Verify, and you and the employer must complete a formal training plan (Form I-983). This is the single biggest practical advantage of a STEM-classified programme in the US, and it is worth checking whether your specific degree code appears on the list before you enrol.

### H-1B and the cap

Longer-term employment usually means an **H-1B** petition filed by an employer. H-1B is subject to an annual cap allocated by a registration and lottery process, which means selection is not a function of how good you or the employer are. Not every employer will sponsor, which is why "will you require sponsorship?" appears on so many applications and why we flag sponsorship on listings.

The official sources are [USCIS on students and employment](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/students-and-exchange-visitors) and [ICE's Study in the States](https://studyinthestates.ice.gov/).

## United Kingdom: Student route and the Graduate route

### Working during study

Student-route visa holders on a degree-level course at a higher education provider with a track record of compliance are typically permitted to work **up to 20 hours a week during term time** and full time outside term. Conditions appear on your visa or BRP/eVisa, and they are the authoritative version for you.

Important restrictions that catch people out: you generally **cannot be self-employed**, cannot work as a professional sportsperson, and cannot take a permanent full-time role. Freelancing — including paid content work or app development as a sole trader — is usually not permitted, even where the hours would be within your limit.

A placement year that is an assessed part of your course is treated differently from ordinary term-time work, which is why sandwich-year placements can be full time.

### The Graduate route

The Graduate route allows eligible students who have completed a qualifying UK degree to stay and work — including self-employment — **without a sponsor and without needing a job offer**. At the time of writing it runs for two years after an undergraduate or master's degree, and three years after a PhD.

Two things to know. It is a one-time, non-extendable route: you cannot renew it, and time on it does not count towards settlement. And it has been the subject of repeated government review, with proposals to change its length and conditions. Check [gov.uk's Graduate visa page](https://www.gov.uk/graduate-visa) for the current position before you build a plan around a specific duration.

For longer-term work you generally need the **Skilled Worker** route, which requires a licensed sponsor and a role meeting salary and skill thresholds. Those thresholds have risen significantly in recent years, though reduced rates apply to some new entrants and new graduates — worth checking, because it changes which roles are realistically open to you.

## Canada: study permits, co-op permits and the PGWP

### Working during study

Study-permit holders who meet the eligibility conditions — enrolled full time at a designated learning institution in an eligible programme, among others — may generally work off campus without a separate work permit, subject to an hours cap during academic sessions. That cap has been changed more than once in recent years, so confirm the current figure on the IRCC site rather than relying on what a classmate did two years ago.

### Co-op work permits

If work experience is a **required part** of your programme, you normally need a separate **co-op or intern work permit** in addition to your study permit. This is a distinct application, it takes time, and your institution must confirm the placement is a required component. Start it well before the term begins — co-op offices usually have a deadline calendar for exactly this reason.

### PGWP — Post-Graduation Work Permit

The PGWP is an **open work permit**, meaning it is not tied to a single employer, which makes it unusually flexible compared with equivalents elsewhere. Its length is linked to the length of your programme, up to a maximum of three years.

Eligibility has tightened considerably. Requirements now include graduating from an eligible programme at a designated learning institution, and — depending on the credential type — **field-of-study requirements** for some non-degree programmes and **language proficiency** thresholds. There are also deadlines after graduation within which you must apply.

Because the field-of-study rules can make the difference between a three-year permit and none at all, check eligibility for your *specific programme* on [IRCC's PGWP page](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/study-canada/work/after-graduation.html) **before** you enrol, not after you graduate.

PGWP time is often used to accumulate the skilled work experience that supports a permanent residence application through Express Entry or a Provincial Nominee Program.

## How to read job adverts with this in mind

Across all three countries, the practical skill is screening roles quickly so you stop spending applications on jobs you cannot take.

- **Search for the sponsorship signal, not just the job title.** Many adverts state their position explicitly. Where we have that information, we flag it on the listing, and you can [filter for sponsorship directly](/jobs?sponsorship=true)
- **Treat "must have unrestricted right to work" as a hard no.** It is not a negotiation; it usually reflects a decision made above the hiring manager
- **Government, defence and security-cleared roles** frequently require citizenship or permanent residence. This is a legal constraint on the employer
- **Be straightforward when asked.** Application forms ask about work authorisation because they must. Answering inaccurately is grounds for withdrawing an offer at any later point, including after you start
- **Know your own timeline before you interview.** Being able to say "I hold a Graduate visa valid until September 2028, so no sponsorship is needed now" removes the employer's main objection immediately. Vagueness creates doubt where none is warranted

## The mistakes that cost people the most

1. **Starting work before authorisation is in place.** CPT must be authorised first; OPT needs the EAD in hand; a Canadian co-op permit must be issued. Unauthorised work is a status violation with serious consequences, and an eager employer saying "just start Monday" is not a defence.
2. **Missing a filing window.** Post-completion OPT and PGWP both have deadlines tied to your programme end date. These are rarely recoverable.
3. **Running up full-time CPT without counting.** Hitting twelve months removes OPT entirely.
4. **Assuming freelancing is fine.** On a UK Student visa, self-employment generally is not permitted regardless of hours.
5. **Relying on a classmate's experience.** Rules have changed repeatedly in all three countries. What applied to the cohort above you may not apply to you.
6. **Not telling the international office about a job change.** Reporting obligations exist on OPT and elsewhere, and quiet non-reporting is a common way people drift out of compliance.

## The one habit worth building

Book a single appointment with your international student office early — in your first term if possible, and again at least six months before you graduate. They deal with these rules daily, they know your institution's specific processes and deadlines, and they will tell you things no article can, because they can see your record.

Then, when an offer arrives, you will already know what you need and how long it takes. That is usually the difference between accepting it and losing it.
