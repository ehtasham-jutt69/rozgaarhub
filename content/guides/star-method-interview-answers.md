"Tell me about a time you worked in a team." Everybody knows the answer should use STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result. Far fewer people know how the answer is being marked, which is why so many technically correct STAR answers still score badly.

The thing to understand is that a competency interview is usually scored against a rubric. The interviewer has a list of behavioural indicators for the competency being tested, and they are ticking off evidence as you speak. Your job is not to tell a good story. It is to supply specific evidence against those indicators, in an order that makes it easy to record.

## What the interviewer is actually doing

For a graduate or internship interview, the interviewer typically has a grid: the competency, a short list of positive indicators, a short list of negative indicators, and a scale of one to five. Positive indicators for teamwork might be *sought others' input*, *resolved disagreement constructively*, *adapted own approach*, *delivered to a shared deadline*.

Three consequences follow, and they explain most of the marks people drop.

**They need to hear what you personally did.** An answer narrated entirely in "we" supplies no evidence about you. This is the single most common failure, and it is fatal because the interviewer cannot tick anything.

**They need specifics, because vague claims cannot be scored.** "I communicated well with the team" is an assertion. "I set up a shared tracker and a ten-minute standup every other day" is behaviour.

**They are listening for the result.** An answer that ends with the action and never says what happened leaves the strongest indicator unmarked.

## The proportions nobody tells you

STAR is usually drawn as four equal boxes. In a good answer the proportions are nothing like equal. For a two-minute answer, aim for roughly:

| Part | Share | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S**ituation | 15% | 15–20 seconds |
| **T**ask | 10% | 10–15 seconds |
| **A**ction | 60% | 60–75 seconds |
| **R**esult | 15% | 15–20 seconds |

Action is the answer. Everything before it is scene-setting so that the action makes sense, and everything after it is proof the action worked.

Most weak answers invert this: ninety seconds of background about the module, the group, the brief and the timetable, then "so we got it done and got a 2:1". All the scoreable content is in the part they ran out of time for.

### Situation: two sentences, maximum

Give only what is needed to understand the action. Who, where, when, and what was at stake.

> "In my second year I was one of five people on a marketing module group project. We had four weeks to produce a campaign plan, and it was 40% of the module grade."

That is enough. Nobody needs the module code or the names of your teammates.

### Task: your responsibility, not the group's

This is the part most people skip, and skipping it is costly, because Task is where you establish that the action was *yours*.

> "I took on the competitor research and, a week in, the job of getting the group back on schedule — we had agreed a split that was not working and had produced almost nothing."

Now the interviewer knows what you owned.

### Action: the heart of it, in first person and in steps

Three to five discrete things you did, in sequence, with the reasoning where the reasoning is the skill.

> "First I asked each person what was actually blocking them, individually rather than in the group chat, because I suspected two people had taken on parts they were not confident about. That turned out to be right. So I proposed we re-split by strength rather than by equal word count — one person did all the data work, I took research and editing. I set up a shared tracker with named owners and dates, and a ten-minute call every other evening, which was short enough that people actually joined. When one teammate missed two deadlines, I spoke to him privately first rather than raising it with the group, and we agreed to move one section to me and one to another member."

Count the indicators that answer supplies: sought others' input, diagnosed the real problem, adapted the approach, created structure, handled a difficult conversation proportionately, kept to a shared deadline. That is a rubric filling itself in.

Notice the "because" clauses. Stating *why* you chose an action is what separates a three from a five — it demonstrates judgement rather than activity.

### Result: quantified, and with the reflection

End with what changed. Use a number if you have one.

> "We submitted a day early and got 72%, the highest mark of the six groups. Two of us have worked together on things since. What I took from it is that a stalled group is usually a scoping problem rather than a motivation problem, so I now ask what is blocking people much earlier than I used to."

The final sentence is optional in the acronym but valuable in practice. Many rubrics include a "learns from experience" indicator, and a one-line reflection picks it up. Keep it to one line — any longer and it reads as filler.

## Building answers with no work experience

Competency questions do not require a job. They require a situation where you had to do something. Perfectly valid sources:

- **Group coursework** — teamwork, conflict, deadlines, planning
- **Part-time and vacation jobs** — customer handling, pressure, process, reliability
- **Societies and sports** — leadership, budgets, organising, recruiting members
- **Volunteering** — initiative, working with unfamiliar people
- **Personal projects** — self-direction, problem solving, finishing something
- **Family or caring responsibilities** — prioritising, resilience, negotiation
- **Course representative or peer mentoring roles** — representing others, difficult feedback

A well-constructed answer about a 90-member debating society budget beats a vague answer about an internship. The source matters much less than the specificity.

## Prepare a bank, not scripts

Memorising answers to specific questions fails, because the question will be phrased differently and you will try to force your script into it. Prepare **material** instead.

Write up six to eight incidents in STAR form — real things, with the numbers attached. Choose them for variety: one with conflict, one with a tight deadline, one where you led, one where you followed, one that failed, one where you persuaded somebody, one where you learned something technical fast.

Then, in the interview, pick the closest incident and angle the Task and Action to the competency being asked about. The same group project can answer teamwork, conflict, planning, or leadership — you emphasise different actions each time. Six well-documented incidents will cover thirty possible questions.

## Where marks get lost

In roughly the order they cost candidates most:

1. **"We" throughout.** No individual evidence, nothing to score. Narrate your own actions in the first person, and credit the team in the result rather than in the action.
2. **No result.** The answer stops after the action. Always land the outcome.
3. **Situation that eats the clock.** Two sentences. Then move.
4. **No numbers anywhere.** Marks, percentages, headcounts, budgets, timescales — any of these make the answer concrete.
5. **Actions with no reasoning.** A list of steps shows activity; a step with a "because" shows judgement.
6. **Choosing a trivial incident.** An example with nothing at stake caps your score however well it is structured. Pick situations where something could have gone wrong.
7. **Blaming others.** Describing a teammate as lazy reads as a flag about you, not them. Describe the behaviour, not the character, and focus on your response.
8. **Running long.** Over three minutes and the interviewer stops recording and starts waiting. Practise aloud with a timer — most people are startled by how long their answers actually are.

## Rehearsing usefully

Reading your answers silently is almost worthless, because the failure modes are verbal — rambling, losing the thread, the Situation sprawling. Say them out loud, to a timer, ideally recorded once so you hear what you actually sound like.

A reasonable routine for the week before an interview: write up your six incidents, then speak each one aloud twice, timing them to land between ninety seconds and two minutes. Then have someone ask you the questions out of order, so you practise the real skill — selecting and angling an incident under mild pressure rather than reciting a prepared one.

That is the whole method. Prepare material rather than scripts, spend sixty per cent of your airtime on what you personally did, attach a number to the outcome, and say why you made the choices you made. Done that way, STAR stops being a formula you are performing and becomes a straightforward way of giving somebody the evidence they are required to collect.
