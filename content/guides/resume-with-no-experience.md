Almost everybody applying for their first internship has the same problem: the resume template has a section called **Work Experience** and there is nothing to put in it. The usual response is to pad — a paragraph of adjectives at the top, a list of software you once opened, a line about being a motivated team player.

That padding is what actually sinks the application. A recruiter filling a graduate role is not looking for experience you obviously cannot have. They are looking for evidence that you can be given a task and will finish it properly. Coursework, society roles, part-time jobs and personal projects all supply that evidence. You just have to write them up as work rather than as biography.

Here is the structure that does that, in the order a reader actually scans it.

## Start with what the reader needs in the first six seconds

The top third of the page gets read properly. Everything else gets skimmed. So the top third must contain your name, how to reach you, what you are studying, and what you are applying for — nothing else.

A header should be four lines at most:

- **Name**, at a size clearly larger than everything else
- **One line of contact details**: city, email, phone, and a link to a portfolio or GitHub if the work there is finished and presentable
- **Degree, institution, expected graduation date**
- **Nothing else**

Delete your full postal address; nobody posts anything. Delete your date of birth, marital status, nationality and photograph — in the US, UK and Canada these invite discrimination claims, and most employers' recruiting policies tell staff to disregard resumes containing them. Delete the word "Curriculum Vitae" as a title. The reader knows what they are holding.

### The summary line: only if it says something

A personal statement is worth including only if it tells the reader something your resume cannot. Compare:

> A highly motivated and detail-oriented final-year student seeking an opportunity to leverage my skills in a dynamic organisation.

That sentence survives being copied onto any resume on earth, which is precisely why it is worthless. Now this:

> Final-year statistics student. Built and maintain the attendance dashboard used by my university's 400-member athletics union. Looking for a summer analytics internship in London.

The second version is specific, verifiable and positions the application. If you cannot write a sentence that specific, leave the section out entirely and give the space to your projects.

## Put education second — and make it do some work

With no job history, education is your main credential, so it goes directly under the header. But a degree title and a date is an underuse of the space.

Include, where each applies:

- **Degree, institution, expected completion date**
- **Your grade** — GPA, predicted classification, or weighted average — but only if it helps you. There is no obligation to publish a number that hurts you, and in the UK quoting a predicted 2:1 is normal practice
- **Three to five relevant modules**, not all twelve. For a data role: Econometrics, Statistical Computing, Database Systems. This is also where genuine keyword matching happens
- **Your dissertation or final-year project title**, with one line on what you actually did
- **Scholarships, prizes or dean's list**, if any

A relevant module list is the cheapest credibility on the page. It tells the reader you have touched the subject in a structured way, which is exactly what they are trying to establish.

## Rename "Work Experience" to something you can fill

This is the real unlock. The section does not have to be called Work Experience. Use whichever of these you can populate, in order of how relevant it is to the role:

- **Projects**
- **Relevant Experience**
- **Leadership & Activities**
- **Employment**

A project section is legitimate experience. A retail job is legitimate experience. A society treasurer role is legitimate experience. What makes them count is how you describe each one.

### The line that works

Every bullet should answer three questions: what did you do, how did you do it, and what changed as a result. Action, method, outcome.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| Worked as a barista | Served 200+ customers per shift in a four-person team; trained two new starters on the till system |
| Group project on machine learning | Built a churn-prediction model in Python (scikit-learn) with three teammates; reached 0.81 AUC and presented findings to the module convenor |
| Treasurer of the debating society | Managed a £4,200 annual budget for a 90-member society; moved reconciliation to a shared spreadsheet, cutting month-end from two hours to fifteen minutes |
| Volunteered at a charity shop | Processed and priced donated stock for a Cancer Research UK branch; covered Saturday shifts unsupervised for eight months |

Notice what is doing the work in the right-hand column. It is not longer words — it is numbers, named tools, and a stated result. The barista line now demonstrates volume, teamwork and training others. The charity line demonstrates reliability over time without supervision, which is the single thing an employer most wants to know about a first hire.

### Part-time and unrelated jobs are worth keeping

There is a persistent belief that a supermarket or hospitality job looks bad on a graduate resume. It does not. It demonstrates punctuality, customer handling and working under pressure, and recruiters read it as evidence you can hold down a commitment alongside study. Keep it — just describe it as work, with the method and the outcome, rather than as a list of duties.

## Skills: specific, honest, and graded

A skills section should be scannable in two seconds and should not contain anything you would not want to be tested on in the interview.

Group them:

- **Technical**: Python (pandas, scikit-learn), SQL, Excel including PivotTables and INDEX/MATCH, Git
- **Languages**: Urdu (native), English (fluent), French (intermediate, DELF B1)

Two rules. First, be specific — "Python (pandas, scikit-learn)" is a claim; "programming" is not. Second, do not list a skill you cannot demonstrate. If your resume says SQL, expect to write a join on a whiteboard. Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose an interview you were otherwise going to pass.

Delete the rating bars. A row of five dots where you have filled in three communicates nothing — three out of five by whose standard? — and it consumes a surprising amount of page width.

## What to cut

Space on a one-page resume is the scarcest thing you have. These earn their deletion:

1. **"References available upon request."** Assumed. If references are wanted you will be asked.
2. **Soft-skill adjectives with no evidence.** "Excellent communication skills" is an assertion. "Presented findings to the module convenor" is evidence. Keep the evidence, cut the assertion.
3. **Microsoft Word and email as skills.** These read as padding and date the resume badly.
4. **Secondary school detail**, once you are in your second year of university, beyond a single line if your grades were strong.
5. **Hobbies, unless they are load-bearing.** "Reading, music, travelling" says nothing. "Maintain a 2,000-subscriber newsletter on urban transit policy" says a great deal and belongs in Projects instead.

## One page, and the formatting that survives

For a first job, one page. Two pages invite the reader to find the point at which you started padding.

On layout, the constraint that matters most is machine readability — most mid-size and large employers run applications through an applicant tracking system before a human reads them, and parsing is where good resumes get mangled. Single column, standard section headings, real text rather than text inside images, and no critical information in headers, footers or text boxes. ## Tailor the top third, not the whole document

Rewriting a resume from scratch for every application is unsustainable and you will stop doing it by the fifth application. Instead, build one strong master resume and change only three things per role:

1. **The summary line**, to name the specific role and company
2. **The order of your Projects or Relevant Experience bullets**, so the most relevant sits first
3. **Three or four terms in your Skills and Modules lists**, matched to the words the job advert actually uses

That is a ten-minute edit, which means you will keep doing it. A targeted top third with a consistent body beats a fully rewritten resume you only managed for two applications.

## A working checklist

Before you send anything, confirm:

- It fits on one page with margins of at least 1.5cm and body text no smaller than 10pt
- Your name and email appear in the body text, not only in a header
- Every bullet begins with a past-tense verb and contains either a number or a named tool
- Nothing on the page is an unevidenced adjective
- The file is named sensibly — `Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf`, not `resume-final-v3.pdf`
- You have read it aloud once, which catches tense slips and missing words that silent proofreading does not

The goal is not a resume that describes you comprehensively. It is a resume that gives a busy reader, in under a minute, a concrete reason to put you in the interview pile. Specifics do that. Adjectives do not.
